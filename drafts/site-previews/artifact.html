How I got here

Sheet 02 · Story

I didn't come in through a computer science degree. After a Communication Studies degree from the University of Minnesota and a stint in insurance sales, I took an installation technician job at Insite Software just to get inside a technology company.

Tier 2 and 3 support taught me how the whole system fit together: printer connectivity, broken customizations, bad data. Volunteering for small Progress customizations to offload the senior engineers turned into a programming job, earned by delivering projects on time and under budget. When the product was rebuilt in C#, I taught myself at night and designed the bridges between the two systems so expensive carrier certifications could be reused across both.

That habit of getting into a system, breaking it down to its pieces, and making sense of it carried me through Epicor and ProAg Crop Insurance and into FMG, where I went from senior engineer on the website team, to team lead, to co-founding the Architecture team that now guides the flagship product.

I still use the communication degree every day. Most of the job at this level is translating: between engineers and product owners, between a technical trade-off and a business decision, between what a system does and what engineering leadership needs to know about it.

Father and husband. I 3D print, do woodworking, train Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu, hike and geocache with my family, and walk my dog Oliver. I like balancing making things with my hands and making them digitally.

How I work

  • Software is a team sport. I do my best work in low-ego rooms where nobody cares whose idea it was.
  • Performance is a feature. Working well means working fast. Tune first. Caching needs approval.
  • Disagree, decide, get in line. Argue the technical case fully, then commit. No relitigating afterward, on either side.
  • Eliminate process before automating it. I don't want to spend tokens on something we shouldn't be doing in the first place.
  • Create over consume. Shipping code teaches faster than reading about it.
  • Scratch your own itch. Tools built for your own problem tend to become solutions for someone else's.

Problems I've solved

Sheet 03 · Problem types

The specifics change from company to company. The shape of the problem usually doesn't.

  • P-01

    Decouple a monolith so teams can release independently

    Problem
    One shared codebase, one release train, every team waiting on everyone else.
    Approach
    Find the seams along team ownership, stand up containerized services with clear contracts, and end the central release.
    Proof
    Leading this now for a 40+ engineer department, .NET to containerized services.
  • P-02

    Get an engineering org productive with AI

    Problem
    New tools, no shared way of using them, and leadership asking what the spend returns.
    Approach
    Workshops per team, weekly trainings, layered repository context, custom agents and skills, and standards the company can measure against.
    Proof
    Six product teams and 40+ engineers over two years. Piloted a vendor modernization agent with Microsoft.
  • P-03

    Stand up an API platform that performs without caching

    Problem
    One internal-only API and no conventions for the next ten.
    Approach
    RESTful design, resources named early, return exactly what the caller needs, tune before you cache.
    Proof
    Three platform APIs founded or built. No data cache needed to date.
  • P-04

    Ship a large product change with a boring release

    Problem
    A core editing experience had to change under a shortened timeline, with a big audience watching.
    Approach
    Prove it first, write forward and backward migrations, rehearse the release so rollback is a non-event.
    Proof
    In-place visual editing for a CMS. Engineering leadership called the release "super boring."
  • P-05

    Replace a front-end framework without stopping the product

    Problem
    An aging framework and no agreed path forward.
    Approach
    Run competing proofs of concept on real features, pick a component foundation, and standardize so the rest of the product can follow.
    Proof
    Knockout.js to React and Material UI. Most of the product has since moved.
  • P-06

    Retire legacy UI with a distributed team

    Problem
    Legacy pages that had to go, with the delivery team in another time zone.
    Approach
    Skip the intermediate stack, go straight to the target architecture, and run the work through tickets, review, testing, and releases.
    Proof
    About a year of ASP.NET Web Forms to React and APIs with an offshore team.
